Section 3.1

What is Central Tendency?

When you have a large set of data, a single number that represents the "centre" or "typical value" of all observations is incredibly useful. This is called a measure of central tendency.

Definition

Central Tendency is the tendency of observations to cluster around a central value in a data set. A measure of central tendency is a single value that represents the entire data set and indicates where the data tends to concentrate.

Also Known As

Measures of central tendency are also called measures of location or averages. In everyday language, when someone says "the average," they usually mean the Arithmetic Mean.

Two important points to remember about any measure of central tendency:

  1. It should be somewhere within the range of the data — not outside it
  2. It should remain unchanged if the observations are rearranged in a different order

Section 3.2

Criteria for a Good Average

Not every average is equally useful. A satisfactory average should meet most of the following 6 conditions:

① Rigorously Defined
Has a clear, precise mathematical formula — no ambiguity
② Based on All Observations
Uses every single value in the data set
③ Simple to Understand
Easy to interpret and explain to non-statisticians
④ Easy to Calculate
Can be computed quickly without complex mathematics
⑤ Amenable to Math Treatment
Can be used in further statistical calculations and algebra
⑥ Stable in Sampling
Should not fluctuate wildly from sample to sample
⑦ Not Affected by Extreme Values
Unusually large or small values shouldn't distort it

Section 3.3

Types of Averages

There are five main types of averages covered in your syllabus:

Arithmetic Mean (AM)
The most common average — sum of all values divided by count. Mathematical in character.
Geometric Mean (GM)
The nth root of the product of all n values. Used for ratios and rates of change.
Harmonic Mean (HM)
Reciprocal of the average of reciprocals. Used for averaging speeds and rates.
Median
The middle value that divides ordered data into two equal halves. A positional average.
Mode
The most frequently occurring value. Can have more than one mode or no mode.
Memory Trick

The first three (AM, GM, HM) are mathematical averages that indicate the magnitude of values. The Median indicates middle position. The Mode indicates the most frequent value.

Section 3.4

Arithmetic Mean

The arithmetic mean (or simply "the mean") is the most widely used average. It is what most people mean when they say "average."

Definition

The Arithmetic Mean is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations.

x̄ = Σxᵢ / nSample Mean (denoted by x̄, Latin letter)
μ = Σxᵢ / NPopulation Mean (denoted by μ, Greek letter)
Population vs Sample Mean

Population Mean (μ): Calculated from all N observations. A fixed, constant value.
Sample Mean (x̄): Calculated from a sample of n observations. A variable — different samples give different means.
The sample mean is used to estimate the population mean.

Example 3.1 From the Book — Simple Mean

Marks of 9 students: 45, 32, 37, 46, 39, 36, 41, 48, 36

Solution

x̄ = (45 + 32 + 37 + 46 + 39 + 36 + 41 + 48 + 36) / 9
x̄ = 360 / 9 = 40 marks

Practice Calculate the Mean

Heights of 6 students (in cm): 160, 175, 163, 158, 170, 182

Solution

x̄ = (160 + 175 + 163 + 158 + 170 + 182) / 6 = 1008 / 6 = 168 cm

When to Use It

A Weighted Mean is used when different observations have different levels of importance. Each value xᵢ is multiplied by a weight wᵢ that reflects its relative importance.

x̄w = Σ(wᵢ × xᵢ) / ΣwᵢUsed for index numbers, birth/death rates, exam grades with different credits

Section 3.4.2

Properties of the Arithmetic Mean

The arithmetic mean has four important mathematical properties you must know:

PropertyStatementIn Simple Words
① Sum of Deviations = 0 Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0 The sum of all differences from the mean is always zero. The mean is the "balance point."
② Minimal Property Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ≤ Σ(xᵢ − a)² for any a The sum of squared deviations is minimum when measured from the mean (not from any other value).
③ Combined Mean x̄ = (n₁x̄₁ + n₂x̄₂ + …) / (n₁ + n₂ + …) The overall mean of combined groups is a weighted average of the group means.
④ Linear Transformation If y = ax + b, then ȳ = ax̄ + b If every observation is changed by a linear formula, the mean changes by the same formula.
Verify Property ① Sum of Deviations = 0

Data: 45, 32, 37, 46, 39, 36, 41, 48, 36. Mean x̄ = 40

Deviations from mean: (45−40) + (32−40) + (37−40) + (46−40) + (39−40) + (36−40) + (41−40) + (48−40) + (36−40)

Verification

= 5 + (−8) + (−3) + 6 + (−1) + (−4) + 1 + 8 + (−4) = 0 ✓

Section 3.4.3

Mean from Grouped Data

When data is organized into a frequency distribution, we use the class marks (midpoints) to represent each class and multiply by the frequency.

x̄ = Σ(fᵢ × xᵢ) / Σfᵢ = Σ(f × x) / nwhere xᵢ = class mark, fᵢ = frequency, n = total frequency
Why is this an Approximation?

In grouped data, we assume all observations in a class are equal to the class midpoint. This introduces a small grouping error, but statistical experience shows these errors are usually small and tend to cancel out.

Section 3.4.4 ★ In Your Syllabus

Change of Origin and Scale

Important — This Topic is in Your Syllabus!

Change of Origin and Scale is explicitly included by your teacher. Learn this method well — it saves huge amounts of calculation time on the exam.

When numbers are large or messy, we can simplify the mean calculation using a coding method. The idea is to shift and scale the data to smaller numbers, compute the mean, then reverse the transformation.

The Transformation

Let a = assumed mean (usually the midpoint with the highest frequency)
Let h = class width (scale factor)
Define coded variable: u = (x − a) / h

Then: x̄ = a + h × ū where ū = Σ(f × u) / n

x̄ = a + h · (Σfᵢuᵢ / n)where uᵢ = (xᵢ − a) / h is the coded value for each class
Example 3.5 Change of Origin and Scale — Apple Weights

Using a = 114.5 (highest frequency midpoint), h = 20 (class width):

WeightMidpoint (x)fu = (x−114.5)/20f × u
65 – 8474.59−2−18
85 – 10494.510−1−10
105 – 124114.51700
125 – 144134.510+1+10
145 – 164154.55+2+10
165 – 184174.54+3+12
185 – 204194.55+4+20
Total60+24
Solution

ū = Σ(f × u) / n = 24 / 60 = 0.4
x̄ = a + h × ū = 114.5 + 20 × 0.4 = 114.5 + 8.0 = 122.5 grams ✓
(Same answer as the direct method — but much less work!)

Why This Works — Key Insight

The mean changes with both change of origin (adding/subtracting a constant) and change of scale (multiplying/dividing). So:
— Adding a constant to all values → mean increases by that constant
— Multiplying all values by a constant → mean gets multiplied by that constant
This is Property ④ (Linear Transformation) in action.

Section 3.5

Geometric Mean (GM)

Definition

The Geometric Mean of n positive values is the nth root of their product.

G = ⁿ√(x₁ × x₂ × … × xₙ)Ungrouped Data (x must be positive)
log G = (1/n) Σ log xᵢPractical Formula using Logarithms
How to Calculate (Using Logs)

Since taking the nth root of a large product is impractical:
1. Take log of each value: log(x₁), log(x₂), …
2. Find the arithmetic mean of the logs
3. Take anti-log of that mean → that's the GM
In short: GM = Anti-log [ (Σ log x) / n ]

For grouped data: log G = (1/n) Σ fᵢ log xᵢ

When to Use GM

Use GM when averaging ratios, rates of change, or percentages — e.g., average population growth rate, average interest rates, price index numbers. Never use GM when any value is zero or negative.

Section 3.6

Harmonic Mean (HM)

Definition

The Harmonic Mean is the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals of the values.

H = n / Σ(1/xᵢ)where n = number of values and xᵢ ≠ 0
When to Use HM — The Golden Rule

"When rates are expressed as x per y, and x is constant, the Harmonic Mean is required; but if y is constant, the Arithmetic Mean is required."

Classic Example: A car travels at 15 km/hr, 20 km/hr, 25 km/hr for equal distances. Distance is constant → Use Harmonic Mean.
But if it travels for equal times at those speeds, use Arithmetic Mean.

Classic Speed Problem Harmonic Mean for Equal Distances

A car travels 30 km at 15 km/hr, next 30 km at 20 km/hr, last 30 km at 25 km/hr. Find average speed.

Solution — Harmonic Mean (distance = constant)

H = 3 / (1/15 + 1/20 + 1/25)
H = 3 / (0.0667 + 0.0500 + 0.0400)
H = 3 / 0.1567 = ≈ 19.15 km/hr

For grouped data: H = n / Σ fᵢ(1/xᵢ)

Relationship: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM

For any set of positive numbers, the Arithmetic Mean is always greater than or equal to the Geometric Mean, which is always greater than or equal to the Harmonic Mean. Equality holds only when all values are equal.

Section 3.7

Median

Definition

The Median is the value that divides an ordered data set into two equal halves — 50% of data lies below it, 50% above it. It is called the "average of position."

Median for Ungrouped Data

Step 1: Arrange data in ascending (or descending) order.
Step 2: Count n (total observations).

If n is ODD
  • Median = the ((n+1)/2)th value in the ordered data
  • Example: n=9 → Median = 5th value
If n is EVEN
  • Median = average of (n/2)th and (n/2 + 1)th values
  • Example: n=10 → Median = average of 5th and 6th values
Example 3.11 From the Book — Median of 9 Values

Marks: 45, 32, 37, 46, 39, 36, 41, 48, 36
Ordered: 32, 36, 36, 37, 39, 41, 45, 46, 48

Solution

n = 9 (odd) → Median = ((9+1)/2)th = 5th value = 39 marks

Median for Grouped Data (Interpolation Formula)

Median = l + (h/f) × (n/2 − C)l = lower boundary of median class, f = frequency of median class, h = class width, C = cumulative frequency before median class, n = total frequency

Steps to Find Median from Grouped Data:

  1. Compute cumulative frequencies
  2. Find n/2 (half the total frequency)
  3. Find the Median Class — the class where cumulative frequency first reaches or exceeds n/2
  4. Apply the interpolation formula

Section 3.7.1

Quantiles: Quartiles, Deciles, Percentiles

What are Quantiles?

Quantiles are values that divide an ordered data set into equal parts. The Median divides data into 2 equal parts. We extend this idea further.

Quartiles (Q)
3 values (Q₁, Q₂, Q₃) dividing data into 4 equal parts. Q₁ = 25%, Q₂ = 50% (=Median), Q₃ = 75%
Deciles (D)
9 values (D₁ to D₉) dividing data into 10 equal parts. D₅ = Median.
Percentiles (P)
99 values (P₁ to P₉₉) dividing data into 100 equal parts. P₅₀ = Median = Q₂ = D₅.
Useful Equivalences

Q₁ = P₂₅ = 25th percentile
Q₂ = D₅ = P₅₀ = Median = 50th percentile
Q₃ = P₇₅ = 75th percentile
D₆ = P₆₀ = 60th percentile

Quartile Formulas for Grouped Data

Q₁ = l + (h/f)(n/4 − C)Lower (First) Quartile
Q₃ = l + (h/f)(3n/4 − C)Upper (Third) Quartile
Example 3.13 Continued Quartiles for Examination Marks

Using the same data (n=905, see above):

Q₁ Calculation

n/4 = 226.25 → Q₁ class: 49.5–59.5 (CF = 285 ≥ 226.25)
l = 49.5, f = 190, h = 10, C = 95
Q₁ = 49.5 + (10/190)(226.25 − 95) = 49.5 + (10/190)(131.25) = 49.5 + 6.9 = 56.4 ≈ 56 marks

Q₃ Calculation

3n/4 = 678.75 → Q₃ class: 69.5–79.5 (CF = 800 ≥ 678.75)
l = 69.5, f = 211, h = 10, C = 589
Q₃ = 69.5 + (10/211)(678.75 − 589) = 69.5 + (10/211)(89.75) = 69.5 + 4.25 = 73.75 ≈ 74 marks

Finding Quantiles in Ungrouped Data

For ungrouped/discrete data with n observations:
— If jn/100 is NOT a whole number: Pⱼ = observation number [jn/100] + 1
— If jn/100 IS a whole number: Pⱼ = average of observations [jn/100] and [jn/100] + 1
(where [x] means the largest integer ≤ x)

Section 3.8

Mode

Definition

The Mode is the value that occurs most frequently in a data set. It is the most common value. A data set can have:

No Mode
When all values occur with equal frequency (e.g., 2, 3, 4, 5 — each appears once)
Unimodal
One mode — one value occurs more than all others
Bimodal
Two modes — two values tie for highest frequency
Multimodal
More than two modes — three or more values tie

Mode for Ungrouped Data

Simply find the value that appears most often. For the assistants data (Example 3.12), x=4 has frequency 10 — highest — so Mode = 4.

Mode for Grouped Data (Modal Class Formula)

The Modal Class is the class with the highest frequency. The mode lies within this class.

Mode = l + [f_m − f₁ / (f_m − f₁) + (f_m − f₂)] × hl = lower boundary of modal class, fₘ = frequency of modal class, f₁ = frequency of class before modal class, f₂ = frequency of class after modal class, h = class width
Alternative Formula (Simpler)
Mode = l + [f₂ / (f₁ + f₂)] × h

where f₁ = (fₘ − f_before) and f₂ = (fₘ − f_after)

Example 3.14 From the Book — Mode of Examination Marks

Modal class = 59.5 – 69.5 (highest frequency = 304)

l = 59.5, fₘ = 304, f₁ = 190 (before), f₂ = 211 (after), h = 10

Solution

Mode = l + [(fₘ − f₁) / (fₘ − f₁) + (fₘ − f₂)] × h
= 59.5 + [(304 − 190) / (304 − 190) + (304 − 211)] × 10
= 59.5 + [114 / (114 + 93)] × 10
= 59.5 + [114/207] × 10
= 59.5 + 5.5 = 65.3 ≈ 65 marks

Practice Find the Mode

Frequency distribution: 20–29 (f=3), 30–39 (f=8), 40–49 (f=15, modal), 50–59 (f=10), 60–69 (f=4)

Solution

Modal class = 40–49. l = 39.5, fₘ = 15, f₁ = 8, f₂ = 10, h = 10
Mode = 39.5 + [(15−8) / (15−8)+(15−10)] × 10
= 39.5 + [7 / (7+5)] × 10
= 39.5 + 5.83 = 45.33

Section 3.9

Empirical Relation between Mean, Median and Mode

For a unimodal, moderately skewed distribution, Karl Pearson found an approximate relationship between the three averages:

Mean − Mode ≈ 3(Mean − Median)Rearranged: Mode ≈ 3 × Median − 2 × Mean
Symmetrical
Mean = Median = Mode
Positively Skewed (Right)
Mode < Median < Mean
Negatively Skewed (Left)
Mean < Median < Mode
Memory Trick — Skewness Direction

Positive Skew (Right tail): Extreme HIGH values pull the Mean to the right. Mean is "pulled away" from Mode. Order: Mode → Median → Mean (left to right).

Negative Skew (Left tail): Extreme LOW values pull the Mean to the left. Order: Mean → Median → Mode (left to right).

Classic Exam Question Find Mode using Empirical Relation

For a frequency distribution, the mean = 40.5 and the median = 36. Find the mode.

Solution

Mode = 3 × Median − 2 × Mean
Mode = 3 × 36 − 2 × 40.5
Mode = 108 − 81 = 27

Practice Using Empirical Relation

Given: Mean = 50, Mode = 44. Find the Median.

Solution

Mean − Mode = 3(Mean − Median)
50 − 44 = 3(50 − Median)
6 = 150 − 3 × Median
3 × Median = 144
Median = 48

Section 3.10

Box Plots (Box and Whisker Plots)

What is a Box Plot?

A Box Plot is a simple graphical tool that summarizes the distribution of a data set using 5 key values: Minimum, Q₁, Median, Q₃, and Maximum. It shows centre, spread, and skewness at a glance.

Parts of a Box Plot

Schematic Box Plot for Example 3.13 (Exam Marks)

MinQ₁ = 56Median = 65Q₃ = 74Max
← Whisker →← Box (IQR) →← Whisker →
Box
Rectangle from Q₁ to Q₃. Shows the middle 50% of data (IQR = Q₃ − Q₁).
Median Line
A line inside the box at the Median. Shows the center of the data.
Whiskers
Lines extending from the box to the minimum and maximum values.
Interpreting Skew
If one whisker is longer → distribution is skewed in that direction.
Why Use Box Plots?

Box plots are especially useful for comparing two or more distributions side by side. They immediately reveal the median, spread (IQR), and whether the data is symmetric or skewed.

Chapter 3 — Quick Summary for Exam

  • 5 averages: AM, GM, HM, Median, Mode. AM is most common and most mathematically useful.
  • Population mean = μ (Greek, fixed); Sample mean = x̄ (Latin, variable)
  • Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0 always (sum of deviations from mean = zero)
  • Combined mean: x̄ = (n₁x̄₁ + n₂x̄₂ + …) / (n₁ + n₂ + …)
  • Grouped data mean: x̄ = Σ(fx) / n using class midpoints
  • Change of Origin & Scale: x̄ = a + h·ū, where u = (x−a)/h (saves calculation effort)
  • GM uses logarithms: log G = (Σ log x)/n; take anti-log to get G
  • HM for equal-distance speed problems: H = n / Σ(1/x)
  • Median: middle value of ordered data; for grouped: l + (h/f)(n/2 − C)
  • Quantiles: Q divides into 4, D into 10, P into 100 equal parts. All use same formula as median with n/4, n/10, etc.
  • Mode: most frequent value; grouped: l + [(fₘ−f₁)/((fₘ−f₁)+(fₘ−f₂))] × h
  • Empirical: Mode = 3×Median − 2×Mean (for moderate skewness)
  • Symmetrical: Mean=Median=Mode | Positive skew: Mode < Median < Mean | Negative skew: Mean < Median < Mode
  • Box plot uses: Min, Q₁, Median, Q₃, Max. Longer whisker → skewed in that direction.
  • AM ≥ GM ≥ HM (always, for positive numbers; equality when all values are equal)
