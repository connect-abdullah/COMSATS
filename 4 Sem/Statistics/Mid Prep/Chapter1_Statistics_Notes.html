Section 1.1

What is Statistics?

The word Statistics comes from the Latin word status, meaning "political state." Originally, it referred to information useful to the government — like population sizes and army strengths. Today, it has a much broader meaning.

Simple Definition

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, analyzing, and interpreting data so we can make better decisions in the face of uncertainty.

The word "Statistics" is used in three different ways:

Three Meanings of the Word "Statistics"

① Plural Noun
Refers to a set of numerical facts or data.
e.g. "Statistics of road accidents in Pakistan"
② Singular Noun
Refers to the subject/science of dealing with data.
e.g. "Statistics is a useful subject."
③ Statistic
A single number calculated from sample data.
e.g. The sample mean is a statistic.

Characteristics of Statistics

Statistics has 8 important characteristics to keep in mind:

  1. It deals with groups of data, not single individuals.
  2. It deals with numerical information, not opinions alone.
  3. It deals with variability — no two people or objects are exactly alike.
  4. It involves uncertainty — that's why we use probability.
  5. It only covers things that can be counted or measured.
  6. Results are valid on average, not in every single case.
  7. Statistical laws are subject to random causes.
  8. Results can be misleading if data is collected or handled carelessly.

Uses of Statistics

Real Life Where is Statistics used?
Government
Census, economic planning, public health
Medicine
Drug trials, disease rates, hospital data
Business
Sales forecasting, quality control
Education
Exam results, student performance
Sports
Player averages, match predictions
Science
Experiments, research findings

Section 1.1.3

Descriptive vs Inferential Statistics

Statistics is divided into two main branches. Think of it this way:

Analogy

Descriptive statistics = taking a photo of what exists right now.
Inferential statistics = using that photo to predict what will happen next.

Descriptive Statistics
  • Summarizes and describes data you already have
  • Uses graphs, tables, averages
  • No predictions or guesses
  • Answers: "What happened?"
Inferential Statistics
  • Uses sample data to make conclusions about a population
  • Involves probability and estimation
  • Predictions are made with uncertainty
  • Answers: "What will happen?"
Example 1 Cricket Player

Descriptive: A cricket player calculates his average score over the last 20 matches.
Inferential: Based on that average, he estimates his chances of scoring well in the next match.

Key Difference

Descriptive = looking at the past data you have. Inferential = using past data to draw conclusions about the future or a larger group.

Example 2 Grocery Bills

Descriptive: Mrs. Rashid finds her average weekly grocery spend over the past 6 months = Rs. 4,200.
Inferential: She predicts she will spend approximately Rs. 4,200/week next year as well.

Practice Q Which branch is it?

Classify each as Descriptive or Inferential:

  1. COMSATS calculates the average CGPA of all current students.
  2. A doctor uses data from 200 patients to predict the effectiveness of a new drug on all patients.
  3. A teacher records how many students passed the mid-term exam.
  4. A company surveys 500 customers to estimate how satisfied all 50,000 customers are.
Answers

1. Descriptive (summarizing existing data)  |  2. Inferential (predicting for a larger group)  |  3. Descriptive (recording what happened)  |  4. Inferential (using sample to estimate population)

Section 1.1.4

Population vs Sample

Core Concept

A Population is the entire group you want to study.
A Sample is a smaller part of that population that you actually collect data from.

We use a sample because studying an entire population is often too expensive, too slow, or impossible. We collect data from the sample, then use it to draw conclusions about the full population.

Population
  • The whole group of interest
  • Size = N (capital letter)
  • Summary values called Parameters
  • Represented by Greek letters (μ, σ)
Sample
  • A subset of the population
  • Size = n (lowercase letter)
  • Summary values called Statistics
  • Represented by Latin letters (x̄, s)
Example 1.5 From the book – Identify population or sample
Statement Answer
Total absentees of all students in a college last month Population
No. of colour TVs owned by all families in Lahore Population
Monthly salaries of all employees of a company Population
Wheat yield per acre for 5 pieces of land Sample

Tip to Identify

If the statement includes words like "all," "every," "entire" — it's a Population. If it mentions only some members (5 pieces, 50 customers, a survey of 200) — it's a Sample.

Section 1.2

Observations & Variables

Definition

A Variable is any characteristic that varies from one person or object to another. For example, height varies from person to person — so height is a variable.

Variables are classified into two main types:

Quantitative Variable
  • Can be expressed as a number
  • Can be measured or counted
  • Examples: age, weight, income, number of children
Qualitative Variable (Attribute)
  • Cannot be expressed as a number
  • Describes a quality or category
  • Examples: gender, eye colour, religion, education
Memory Trick

Quantitative = Quantity (a number you can measure).
Qualitative = Quality (a category or description).

Section 1.2.2

Discrete vs Continuous Variables

Quantitative variables are further divided into Discrete and Continuous.

Discrete Variable
  • Can only take whole number values
  • Values are counted, not measured
  • There are "gaps" between possible values
  • Example: you can have 2 or 3 children, never 2.5
Continuous Variable
  • Can take any value in a range — including fractions
  • Values are measured, not counted
  • No gaps — infinite possible values between any two points
  • Example: weight could be 60.1, 60.15, 60.152 kg
Simple Rule

Ask yourself: "Does it make sense to have a fraction of this?"
No → Discrete (you can't have 2.5 accidents)
Yes → Continuous (you can weigh 65.7 kg)

Section 1.2.3

Measurement Scales

When we collect data, we assign numbers to observations. Measurement scales tell us exactly how meaningful those numbers are. There are 4 scales, from lowest to highest level of information:

Scale What it does Key feature Examples
Nominal Groups things into categories. Numbers are just labels — they have NO mathematical meaning. No order between categories Gender (1=Male, 2=Female), Eye colour, Religion, Nationality
Ordinal Categories can be ranked or ordered, but the gap between ranks is NOT equal. Ordering exists, but gaps are unequal Grades (A, B, C), Performance (Excellent/Good/Poor), Cricket ranking
Interval Equal gaps between values. But no true zero — zero doesn't mean "nothing." Equal intervals, no true zero Temperature (°C, °F), IQ score, SAT scores
Ratio Equal gaps AND a true zero (zero means "nothing exists"). Ratios are meaningful. Equal intervals + true zero Height, Weight, Age, Salary, Distance, Time
Classic Example — Why Interval has no True Zero

Temperature 0°C does NOT mean there is no temperature — it's just a reference point. That's why you cannot say "40°C is twice as hot as 20°C."

But weight 0 kg truly means no weight — so you CAN say "80 kg is twice as heavy as 40 kg." That's Ratio scale.

Memory Trick — NOIR

Remember the order with NOIR:
Nominal → Ordinal → Interval → Ratio
Each level includes all the features of the levels below it.

Practice Identify the measurement scale

  2. Rankings of squash players (1st, 2nd, 3rd)
  3. Temperature of a city

Section 1.2.4

Errors of Measurement

In real life, we can never measure a continuous variable with perfect accuracy. There is always a small difference between the measured value and the true value. This difference is called an error of measurement.

Note

In statistics, an "error" does not mean a mistake. It simply means a chance inaccuracy that is unavoidable during measurement.

Types of Errors

Biased (Systematic) Error
  • Observed value is consistently higher or lower than the true value
  • Caused by faulty instruments, personal bias
  • Does NOT cancel out — errors accumulate
  • Also called: Cumulative Errors
Unbiased (Random) Error
  • Observations sometimes higher, sometimes lower than true value
  • Occur equally in both directions — tend to cancel out
  • Reduced by repeating measurements
  • Also called: Accidental / Compensating Errors

Types of Error Values

Absolute Error = Measured Value − True Value = ε
Relative Error = Absolute Error ÷ True Value = ε / (x + ε)
Percentage Error = Relative Error × 100
Example Understanding Error

A student's weight is recorded as 60 kg (to the nearest kg).

This means the true weight lies between 59.5 and 60.5 kg.

If the weight is recorded as 60.00 kg (to nearest 0.01 kg), then the true weight lies between 59.995 and 60.005 kg — much more precise!

Takeaway

The more decimal places in your measurement, the smaller the possible error range, and the higher the accuracy.

Section 1.2.5 – 1.2.6

Significant Digits & Rounding

Definition

Significant digits are the digits in a number that carry meaningful, accurate information. They show how precise a measurement actually is.

Rules for Significant Digits

  • 1
    All non-zero digits are significant e.g. 35 has 2 sig. digits; 2.145 has 4 sig. digits
  • 2
    Zeros after decimal point at the END are significant e.g. 2.500 has 4 sig. digits (the trailing zeros matter)
  • 3
    Zeros after decimal point at the BEGINNING are NOT significant e.g. 0.04 has 1 sig. digit; 0.000237 has 3 sig. digits
  • 4
    Zeros within a number (between non-zero digits) are significant e.g. 20.3 has 3; 1001 has 4; 4.00507 has 6 sig. digits
  • 5
    Zeros at the end of whole numbers — ambiguous (use standard notation) e.g. 75400 — could have 3, 4, or 5 sig. digits. Write as 7.54 × 10⁴ (3 sig. digits) or 7.5400 × 10⁴ (5 sig. digits) to be clear.

Rules for Rounding Off

3 Rules How to round a number
Rule 1 — First dropped digit > 5 (or 5 followed by non-zero digits)

Round UP the last kept digit.
e.g. 5.3772 rounded to 3 sig. digits → 5.38 (because 7 > 5)

Rule 2 — First dropped digit < 5

Keep the last digit unchanged.
e.g. 2.1548 rounded to 3 sig. digits → 2.15 (because 4 < 5)

Rule 3 — First dropped digit is exactly 5

If the last kept digit is odd → round up. If even → leave unchanged.
e.g. 4.535 → last kept digit 3 is odd → 4.54
e.g. 2.745 → last kept digit 4 is even → 2.74

Practice Round to 4 significant digits
  1. 32.21705
  2. 937.05002
  3. 0.003599499
  4. 0.07000455
Answers

1. 32.22 (5th digit is 7 > 5, round up)  |  2. 937.1 (5th digit is 5, 4th digit 0 is even → round up)  |  3. 0.003599 (4 sig. digits after leading zeros)  |  4. 0.07000 (the zeros after 7 are significant once you have 4 sig. digits)

Section 1.3

Collection of Data

Primary Data
  • Originally collected by the researcher themselves
  • Raw, unprocessed data — first-hand
  • More accurate but expensive and time-consuming
  • Example: conducting a survey yourself
Secondary Data
  • Data already collected by someone else
  • Has been processed or published before
  • Cheaper and faster, but may be less accurate for your purpose
  • Example: using PBS (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics) reports

Methods of Collecting Primary Data

  • 1
    Direct Personal Investigation Investigator collects data personally by interviewing individuals. Very accurate but costly and slow for large areas.
  • 2
    Indirect Investigation / Personal Interviews Third parties or witnesses are interviewed when direct sources don't exist or are reluctant to respond. Useful for complex information.
  • 3
    Collection through Questionnaires Printed forms sent by mail. Cheap and good for large surveys, but many respondents don't return them. Questions must be simple, short, and clear.
  • 4
    Collection through Enumerators Trained enumerators help fill in forms on behalf of respondents. Most reliable method for large government surveys.
  • 5
    Collection through Local Sources Local agents collect and send data using their own judgment. Cheap and fast but gives only estimates.
  • 6
    Computer Interviews Respondents directly enter data into a computer in response to questions on a screen. Modern and increasingly common.

Sources of Secondary Data

Internal
Data from within the organization itself
Sales reports, accounting records, invoices
Official
Government publications
PBS, Ministry of Finance, Federal Bureau of Statistics
Semi-Official
Public institutions
State Bank of Pakistan, Railway Board
Research Orgs
Universities and journals
Research papers, trade journals, newspapers
Editing of Data

After collecting data, it must be carefully edited — checked for incomplete, inconsistent, or incorrect entries — before analysis begins. For primary data this means checking questionnaires. For secondary data it means verifying the reliability of the source.

Chapter 1 — Quick Summary for Exam

  • Statistics = collecting, organizing, analyzing, and interpreting data
  • Descriptive = describes existing data; Inferential = uses sample to conclude about population
  • Population (N) = entire group; Sample (n) = subset of the population
  • Parameter = summary of population; Statistic = summary of sample
  • Quantitative variables = numerical; Qualitative (Attribute) = non-numerical categories
  • Discrete = counts (whole numbers only); Continuous = measurements (any value)
  • 4 Scales: Nominal → Ordinal → Interval → Ratio (NOIR)
  • Interval: equal gaps, no true zero (Temperature); Ratio: equal gaps + true zero (Weight)
  • Biased errors accumulate; Unbiased errors cancel out
  • Primary data = collected first-hand; Secondary data = already processed by others
  • Rounding rule 3: dropped digit = exactly 5 → round up if last kept digit is odd, keep if even
