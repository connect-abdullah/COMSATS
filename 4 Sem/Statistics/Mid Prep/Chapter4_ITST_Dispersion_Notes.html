Introduction

Why Dispersion Matters — The Big Picture

Imagine two classes both have an average score of 60 out of 100. Sounds the same, right? But what if Class A has scores like 58, 59, 60, 61, 62 — and Class B has scores like 10, 20, 60, 90, 100? The average is the same, but the data are very different.

🎓 Teacher Says

The average (mean, median, mode) tells you where the centre of the data is. But it doesn't tell you how spread out the data is around that centre. That's what Dispersion measures. You need BOTH to properly describe any dataset.

Dispersion

The extent to which observations in a sample or population vary about their mean. A quantity that measures this is called a measure of dispersion, scatter, or variability.

A good measure of dispersion should be: (i) in the same units as the data, (ii) zero when all observations are equal, (iii) independent of origin (shifting data left/right doesn't change spread), (iv) multiplied/divided when the data is scaled.

Absolute Measures
  • Measured in the same units as the data
  • Range, Quartile Deviation, Mean Deviation, Standard Deviation
  • Cannot compare data in different units
Relative Measures
  • Expressed as a ratio, coefficient, or percentage — no units
  • Coeff. of Range, Coeff. of QD, Coeff. of MD, Coeff. of Variation
  • Used to compare data in different units (e.g., rupees vs. kg)

The four main measures you need to learn are: Range, Quartile Deviation, Mean Deviation, and Standard Deviation. Let's take each one carefully.

Section 4.2

The Range

Range (R)

The simplest measure of dispersion. It's just the difference between the largest and smallest values in the data.

R = x_max − x_minx_max = largest value, x_min = smallest value
Coeff. of Dispersion = (x_max − x_min) / (x_max + x_min)A pure number — no units. Used to compare.
📌 When to Use Range

Range is used in: daily temperatures, stock prices, and statistical quality control of products. It's fast to calculate but has one big weakness — it only uses TWO values (the extremes) and ignores everything in between. One outlier can make it misleading.

⚠️ Disadvantages of Range

(1) It ignores ALL the intermediate observations — only the two extremes matter. (2) It is greatly affected by extreme values (outliers). (3) Cannot be computed if there are open-end classes in a frequency distribution.

Section 4.3

Quartile Deviation (Semi-Interquartile Range)

🎓 Understanding Quartile Deviation

Remember Q1 and Q3 from Chapter 3? Q1 is the value that cuts the bottom 25% of data, and Q3 cuts the top 25%. The range from Q1 to Q3 is called the Interquartile Range (IQR) — it covers the middle 50% of data. Half of IQR is the Quartile Deviation.

Q.D. = (Q₃ − Q₁) / 2Semi-Interquartile Range
Coeff. of Q.D. = (Q₃ − Q₁) / (Q₃ + Q₁)Relative measure — no units
💡 Key Fact About QD

The interval "Median ± Q.D." contains approximately 50% of the data. QD is better than Range because it's not affected by extreme values (outliers). It only looks at the middle 50% of the data.

⚠️ Disadvantages of QD

(1) Ignores the bottom 25% and top 25% — no information about those observations. (2) Not amenable to mathematical treatment (can't use it in further calculations easily). (3) Greatly affected by sampling variability.

Section 4.4

Mean Deviation (MD)

🎓 The Idea Behind Mean Deviation

We want to measure how far, on average, each observation is from the centre (mean or median). The problem? If we just add the deviations, they cancel out to zero! So we take the absolute value (ignore the minus sign) of each deviation. Then we average them. That's the Mean Deviation.

M.D. = Σ|xᵢ − x̄| / nFrom the Mean (sample)
M.D. = Σ|xᵢ − Median| / nFrom the Median
For Grouped Data: M.D. = Σ fᵢ|xᵢ − x̄| / nwhere fᵢ = frequency and xᵢ = class midpoint
Coeff. of M.D. = M.D. / MeanOr: M.D. / Median
M.D. is MINIMUM when measured from MedianImportant theorem to remember!
💡 Why We Use Absolute Values

If we take x₁ − x̄ for each observation, the positive and negative values cancel perfectly (sum = 0, by the first property of the mean). To avoid this, we use |xᵢ − x̄| — the absolute deviation — which is always positive regardless of direction.

Section 4.5

Variance & Standard Deviation

🎓 The Most Important Measure

Standard Deviation is the KING of dispersion measures. It uses ALL observations, is mathematically well-behaved, and is the backbone of almost all advanced statistics. The idea: take the deviation of each value from the mean, square it (to make all values positive AND give more weight to large deviations), average the squared deviations — that's the Variance. Take the square root — that's the Standard Deviation.

Variance (σ² or s²)

The mean of the squares of deviations from the mean. Units are squared (e.g., marks²).

σ² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / NPopulation Variance
s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / nSample Variance (divide by n)
s² = [Σxᵢ² − (Σxᵢ)²/n] / n  =  Σxᵢ²/n − (x̄)²Short-cut Formula: "Mean of squares minus square of mean"
s = √s² = √[Σxᵢ²/n − (x̄)²]Standard Deviation = positive square root of Variance

Short Method (Change of Origin and Scale)

When numbers are large, calculations are tedious. We use a coding method:

u = (x − a) / h  →  s = h × √[Σfᵢuᵢ²/n − (Σfᵢuᵢ/n)²]a = assumed mean (usually midpoint of class with highest frequency), h = class width

Section 4.5.3

Coefficient of Variation (CV)

🎓 When Do We Use CV?

Imagine you want to compare the variation in: (1) heights of students (in cm) and (2) weights of students (in kg). You can't compare their standard deviations directly because they have different units! CV solves this by expressing the SD as a percentage of the mean — making it unit-free and comparable across different types of data.

C.V. = (s / x̄) × 100%Sample Coefficient of Variation
C.V. = (σ / μ) × 100%Population Coefficient of Variation
💡 Interpreting CV

• Larger CV = More variation (less consistent)
• Smaller CV = Less variation (more consistent)

In cricket, the player with the smaller CV of scores is more consistent. In quality control, the product with smaller CV has more uniform quality.

Properties of Variance and Standard Deviation

🎓 These Properties Are Heavily Tested

These rules tell you what happens to variance and SD when you add a constant to data, or multiply data by a constant. Very useful in shortcuts!

PropertyRule for VarianceRule for SDSimple Explanation
Var(constant)= 0= 0If all values are the same, there's no spread.
Var(X + a)= Var(X)SD(X+a) = SD(X)Adding a constant SHIFTS data but doesn't change spread.
Var(aX)= a² × Var(X)SD(aX) = |a| × SD(X)Multiplying by a constant SCALES the spread — variance by a², SD by |a|.
Var(X ± Y) — Independent= Var(X) + Var(Y)√[Var(X)+Var(Y)]For independent variables, variances ADD (never subtract)!

Section 4.5.5

Standardized Variables (Z-scores)

🎓 What is a Z-score?

A Z-score tells you: "How many standard deviations is this observation away from the mean?" A student who scored 70 in an exam where mean=60 and SD=5 is (70−60)/5 = 2 standard deviations above average. A student in another exam where mean=60 and SD=20 who scored 70 is only (70−60)/20 = 0.5 SD above — much less impressive! Z-scores let you compare across different scales.

Z = (X − μ) / σPopulation Z-score
Z = (x − x̄) / sSample Z-score
💡 Key Property

For any standardized variable Z: Mean of Z = 0, Variance of Z = 1. This is always true, regardless of what the original distribution looked like. Z-scores have no units.

20
A student scored 75 in Statistics (class mean=65, SD=10) and 80 in Math (class mean=70, SD=20). In which subject did the student perform relatively better?
Show Answer & Solution
1
Z for Statistics = (75−65)/10 = 10/10 = 1.0
2
Z for Math = (80−70)/20 = 10/20 = 0.5
The student performed relatively better in Statistics (Z=1.0 > Z=0.5). The 10-mark gap above the mean was more impressive in Statistics where SD is smaller.

Section 4.6

Trimmed & Winsorized Measures

🎓 The Problem of Outliers

Imagine you have exam scores: 50, 55, 58, 60, 62, 65, 68, 70, 98. That 98 is an outlier — it pulls the mean upward and inflates the SD. When outliers are present, the mean and SD become misleading. We need resistant measures.

Trimmed Data
  • Remove a certain % of smallest AND largest observations
  • Typically: remove values below Q₁ and above Q₃
  • Trimmed Mean = mean of remaining values
  • Trimmed SD = SD of remaining values
  • Effect: outliers are gone completely
Winsorized Data (C.P. Winsor)
  • Replace extreme values — don't remove them
  • Values below Q₁ → replace with Q₁ value
  • Values above Q₃ → replace with Q₃ value
  • Winsorized Mean = mean of modified dataset
  • Effect: outliers are "pulled in" to the boundaries
21
For the data: 32, 36, 36, 37, 39, 41, 45, 46, 48 (where Q₁=36 and Q₃=45), calculate the Trimmed Mean and Winsorized Mean. (From Book — Example 4.12)
Show Answer & Solution
1
Trimmed: Remove values below Q₁=36 (i.e., remove 32 and one 36) and above Q₃=45 (remove 46 and 48). Remaining: 36, 37, 39, 41, 45.
2
Trimmed Mean = (36+37+39+41+45)/5 = 198/5 = 39.6
3
Winsorized: Replace 32 and 36 (below Q₁) with 36, and 46 and 48 (above Q₃) with 45. New dataset: 36, 36, 36, 37, 39, 41, 45, 45, 45.
4
Winsorized Mean = (36+36+36+37+39+41+45+45+45)/9 = 360/9 = 40
Trimmed Mean = 39.6 | Winsorized Mean = 40 (same as original mean here, but SD would differ)

Section 4.7

Moments

🎓 What Are Moments?

In physics, a "moment" is the turning effect of a force. In statistics, a moment is the average of powers of deviations. The rth moment = average of the rth power of deviations from some point. Moments are used to describe the shape of a distribution — its centre, spread, skewness, and peakedness.

Moments About the Mean (Central Moments)

mᵣ = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)ʳrth Sample Central Moment
μᵣ = (1/N) Σ(xᵢ − μ)ʳrth Population Central Moment

Moments About Arbitrary Origin (a)

m'ᵣ = (1/n) Σfᵢ(xᵢ − a)ʳrth Sample Raw Moment
m'ᵣ = (1/n) Σfᵢuᵢʳ × hʳ (short method)where u = (x−a)/h
📌 Special Values of r

• r = 0: m₀ = 1 always (trivial)
• r = 1: m₁ = 0 (mean of deviations from mean = 0 always)
• r = 2: m₂ = s² (the variance — the second central moment is the variance!)
• r = 3: m₃ measures skewness (asymmetry)
• r = 4: m₄ measures kurtosis (peakedness)

Converting Raw Moments to Central Moments

Very Important Formulas

Given moments about arbitrary origin (m'₁, m'₂, m'₃, m'₄), find moments about mean:

m₁ = 0
m₂ = m'₂ − (m'₁)²
m₃ = m'₃ − 3m'₂·m'₁ + 2(m'₁)³
m₄ = m'₄ − 4m'₃·m'₁ + 6m'₂·(m'₁)² − 3(m'₁)⁴

Section 4.7.3

Moment Ratios (β₁ and β₂)

🎓 Why Moment Ratios?

The raw moments (m₂, m₃, m₄) have units (marks², marks³, etc.). To make shape comparisons between distributions unit-free, we form ratios. β₁ and β₂ are the most important.

β₁ = m₃² / m₂³Measure of Skewness (zero for symmetric)
β₂ = m₄ / m₂²Measure of Kurtosis (=3 for normal distribution)
📌 Sample Equivalents

b₁ = m₃² / m₂³ (sample skewness measure) | b₂ = m₄ / m₂² (sample kurtosis measure)

For a normal (bell-shaped) distribution: β₁ = 0, β₂ = 3

Show Answer & Solution
1
b₁ = m₃² / m₂³ = (20.67)² / (25.78)³ = 427.25 / 17130.45 = 0.025
2
b₂ = m₄ / m₂² = 1189.78 / (25.78)² = 1189.78 / 664.62 = 1.79
b₁ = 0.025 (close to 0 — nearly symmetric) | b₂ = 1.79 (less than 3 — platykurtic, flat-topped)

Section 4.8

Skewness

🎓 What is Skewness?

In a perfectly symmetric distribution, the left side is a mirror image of the right side, and the mean = median = mode. Skewness measures how much and in which direction the distribution deviates from symmetry. Think of a tail — which way does it drag?

Symmetrical
Mean = Median = Mode
Sk = 0, β₁ = 0
Positively Skewed (Right tail)
Mode < Median < Mean
Sk > 0, long right tail
Negatively Skewed (Left tail)
Mean < Median < Mode
Sk < 0, long left tail

Measures of Skewness

Sk = (Mean − Mode) / SDKarl Pearson's First Coefficient
Sk = 3(Mean − Median) / SDKarl Pearson's Second Coefficient (when mode unclear)
Sk = (Q₁ + Q₃ − 2·Median) / (Q₃ − Q₁)Bowley's Coefficient (−1 to +1)
Sk = m₃ / s³ = b₁ (or √β₁)Moment-based coefficient (−2 to +2 typically)

Section 4.9

Kurtosis

🎓 What is Kurtosis?

Skewness tells you about asymmetry. Kurtosis tells you about peakedness — how tall and sharp (or flat and wide) the peak of the distribution is. The standard reference is the normal distribution (the bell curve), which has β₂ = 3.

β₂ = μ₄ / μ₂² (population)    or    b₂ = m₄ / m₂² (sample)Measured by the fourth standardized moment. Normal distribution: β₂ = 3
Mesokurtic
β₂ = 3
Normal distribution. The reference shape. Medium peak, medium tails.
Leptokurtic
β₂ > 3
Sharply peaked with heavy/wide tails. More extreme values than normal.
Platykurtic
β₂ < 3
Flat-topped with narrow tails. Fewer extreme values than normal.
💡 Memory Trick

"LEPTO" means thin/narrow in Greek → Leptokurtic has a tall narrow PEAK (and wide tails to compensate).
"PLATY" means flat/broad → Platykurtic has a flat broad top (and narrow tails).
"MESO" means middle → Mesokurtic is the normal distribution — the standard.

Chapter 4 — Complete Summary for Exam

  • Dispersion measures the spread/variability of data around its average. Average alone is insufficient to describe data.
  • Absolute measures: Range, QD, MD, SD — in same units as data. Relative measures (coefficients) — dimensionless, for comparison.
  • Range R = x_max − x_min. Easy but ignores all middle values and is affected by outliers.
  • QD = (Q₃−Q₁)/2. Not affected by extremes. "Median ± QD" contains ~50% of data.
  • MD = Σ|x−x̄|/n (or Σ|x−median|/n). Uses absolute values to avoid cancellation. Minimum when from median.
  • Variance s² = Σ(x−x̄)²/n = Σx²/n − (x̄)². Standard Deviation s = √Var. Units match data.
  • Short method for SD: u=(x−a)/h, then s = h√[Σfu²/n − (Σfu/n)²]
  • Var(a) = 0 | Var(X+a) = Var(X) | Var(aX) = a²Var(X) | Var(X±Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y) for independent
  • CV = (s/x̄)×100%. Dimensionless. Smaller CV = more consistent. Used to compare variation across different units.
  • Z = (x−μ)/σ. Standardized variable: mean=0, variance=1. Used to compare across different scales.
  • Trimmed: remove values outside Q₁–Q₃. Winsorized: replace extreme values with Q₁ or Q₃. Both resist outliers.
  • m₁=0, m₂=variance, m₃ relates to skewness, m₄ relates to kurtosis. Raw→Central: m₂=m'₂−(m'₁)²
  • β₁ = m₃²/m₂³ (skewness). β₂ = m₄/m₂² (kurtosis). Normal: β₁=0, β₂=3.
  • Skewness: Positive → right tail (Mean>Median>Mode). Negative → left tail (Mode>Median>Mean). Zero → symmetric.
  • Pearson's Sk = (Mean−Mode)/SD = 3(Mean−Median)/SD. Bowley's Sk = (Q₁+Q₃−2·Median)/(Q₃−Q₁).
  • Kurtosis: β₂=3 Mesokurtic (normal) | β₂>3 Leptokurtic (peaked, heavy tails) | β₂<3 Platykurtic (flat, light tails).
